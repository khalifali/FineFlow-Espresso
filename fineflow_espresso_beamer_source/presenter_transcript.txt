SLIDE 1
Today I will introduce FineFlow-Espresso as a one-dimensional continuum model of fines migration. The aim is to connect changes in the coffee puck with pressure, flow and fines leaving the puck. The current results demonstrate the numerical model. They are not a calibrated prediction of the experiments. Pore-network modeling is a possible next step for determining closures from the pore geometry.

SLIDE 2
The key distinction is the history of the retained fines. The variable m_a is the remaining releasable inventory that was initially attached to the coffee matrix. It is an evolving state, not just an initial condition. The variable m_d represents fines captured after entering the mobile phase. The current model assigns different release laws to these populations and associates additional blockage with m_d. This is a modeling hypothesis. If experiments do not support the distinction, we should combine them into one retained inventory.

SLIDE 3
The present model considers forward flow and axial variations of cross-section-averaged fields. Reverse flow and radial channeling are explicitly outside the scope of this work. This does not mean radial structure is absent: Mahima's CT observations include radial heterogeneity and blocking clusters. We must compare with measurements to determine whether axial averaging adequately captures the overall hydraulic response. CT images at selected stages are snapshots, not continuous histories. Time-resolved cup-fines measurements remain a proposed input with unconfirmed availability. Pore-network and multidimensional continuum models are possible future extensions.

SLIDE 4
Consider an infinitesimally thin slice through the puck with cross-sectional area A and thickness dz. Within this volume, the water-filled pores contain mobile fines, while the stationary skeleton carries retained fines. Advection and dispersion act across the two axial boundaries. Release, capture and remobilization exchange fines locally between the two phases. Taking the slice thickness to zero gives the continuum equations. Finite-volume cells are a later numerical choice. Extraction chemistry is outside this model; its hydraulic relevance remains an assumption to assess.

SLIDE 5
The global balance refers to a specific accounting domain: the entire puck, the fines retained by the basket, and the collected fines in the cup. Capital M denotes actual mass. In particular, mobile mass is the integral of epsilon times c over the puck; epsilon times c alone is a mass density. Local transfers cancel when the inventories are added. With clean inlet water and no creation or uncounted loss of fines, the total equals the initial fines mass.

SLIDE 6
These are the balance equations before choosing a grid or numerical method. The mobile phase gains fines from initial release and deposit remobilization, and loses fines by capture. Heiko's surface-loading formulation is equivalent: define sigma as accessible surface area per bulk volume and c_a as mass per unit surface. Then m_a equals sigma times c_a. If sigma changes, its derivative must remain inside the time derivative. The existing solver stores bulk mass densities; introducing measured surface area would clarify parameter interpretation without requiring a new conservation law.

SLIDE 7
These equations are closures, not consequences of mass conservation. The release law assumes that stronger flow increases the release rate and that the remaining available inventory limits release. The capture coefficient has units of inverse length because it multiplies a mass flux to give a volumetric rate. The capacity factor reduces capture as deposits accumulate. The optional axial weighting should not impose a basket-side blocking region without supporting evidence. We can replace these laws without changing the conservation framework.

SLIDE 8
The word escape was ambiguous. Here it means local access to the flowing pore space after a deposit is disturbed. It does not mean escape from the entire puck. The model multiplies a potential detachment rate by an effective accessibility factor. The unsuccessful fraction remains in the deposit inventory; we do not resolve a separate detached-but-trapped state. Once mobile, fines can be captured again downstream. A mean hydraulic diameter is only a proxy for constrictions. The logistic law needs evidence and could be removed if it adds unidentifiable parameters.

SLIDE 9
The particle-size distribution provides an estimate of the fine fraction and representative diameters. A diameter cutoff alone does not prove that every particle below it is releasable. An availability fraction or independent release measurement is needed to initialize m_a. The two curves are schematic. Specific surface area should preferably be measured or estimated consistently from the distribution and particle shape. A pore-network calculation could later help connect particle size to accessibility, but it is not a current data source.

SLIDE 10
These are alternative closures for the same permeability, and either can be used with Darcy flow. Kozeny-Carman is our structural baseline: the implemented ratio assumes fixed effective particle scale and Kozeny factor while porosity changes. The exponential relation instead assigns permeability directly from deposited mass. It was introduced as an empirical modeling assumption, not derived from our coffee measurements. Our comparison keeps initial permeability identical, so it tests the evolving relative permeability rather than independently predicting the initial permeability. Neither closure has been validated against the experiments. The displayed exponential results are retained as a sensitivity comparison.

SLIDE 11
The motivation for the exponential law was to allow strong resistance growth when fines obstruct narrow flow paths, even if the total void volume changes relatively little. That is a physical hypothesis, but the exponential equation itself does not resolve those paths. It was chosen for a simple, smooth, positive response with an adjustable coefficient. The coefficient 5.5 was illustrative and not fitted to our data or taken from a cited coffee study. At half the deposit capacity, the formula already reduces permeability to about 6.4 percent of its initial value. At full capacity it gives about 0.41 percent before numerical bounds. Thus the strong blockage in the original result is substantially imposed by the chosen closure. We should treat Kozeny-Carman as the structural baseline and the exponential law as an empirical sensitivity case until measurements can justify it.

SLIDE 12
Darcy and Kozeny-Carman do different jobs. Darcy relates pressure gradient to flow through a material with permeability K. Kozeny-Carman estimates that permeability from structure. They are therefore used together. Forchheimer is an optional extension of the flow law when inertial pressure loss becomes relevant. A pore-network model can also supply permeability for Darcy flow; using PNM does not itself require Forchheimer. The integral gives the total puck resistance for the one-dimensional approximation.

SLIDE 13
We ran the same case twice, changing only the flow law. One run includes the Forchheimer inertial term and the other uses Darcy alone. The permeability closure, initial state, kinetics and PI controller settings are identical. At sixty seconds, pressure, flow rate and collected cup fines are the same to the precision displayed in the table. They are not mathematically identical: the values have been rounded. Across the entire simulation, the largest pressure difference is only 0.0203 pascals. We therefore choose Darcy for this tested case because including inertia makes no meaningful difference to these predictions.

SLIDE 14
Basket retention and basket hydraulic resistance are different effects. The resistance determines its pressure drop, while eta determines how much of the exiting fines mass stays in the basket. A constant-pressure experiment does not need a controller model. For a measured machine transient, either impose the measured boundary history or characterize the controller. This implementation uses PI, not PID, plus a pump lag and flow cap. Its gains are illustrative unless independently determined. Basket loading does not yet change basket resistance.

SLIDE 15
The clean-water inlet supplies no fines, so we specify zero total incoming fines flux. This is a flux boundary condition; it does not mean imposing both zero concentration and zero concentration gradient on the interior solution. At the outlet, we neglect dispersive exchange with the downstream region and let advection carry fines out. The code omits the dispersive contribution at boundary faces. These choices close the truncated puck domain without modeling a downstream concentration field. They are assumptions, not a requirement of finite volumes. If downstream mixing or back-dispersion affects the result, the boundary model must be revisited. Initial fines fields remain necessary, and basket resistance sets the outlet pressure relative to the cup.

SLIDE 16
Finite volumes balance the fines inventory in each cell through shared face fluxes. The flux that leaves one cell enters its neighbor, so interior exchanges cancel in the global sum. The code uses first-order upwind advection and centered dispersion. It does not need a high-order advection flux limiter, but numerical diffusion can smear steep concentration fronts. We therefore assess spatial refinement. Positivity-preserving local reaction transfers are a separate issue from transport flux limiting. The next slide explains how the timestep is selected.

SLIDE 17
The solver takes the smallest of four limits. The user maximum controls the largest permitted step. The transport estimate combines advection at the largest pore velocity with explicit dispersion and uses a safety factor of 0.42. In PI mode, another bound takes one quarter of the pump response time. This resolves the actuator response and is not a bound based on the PI integral time. The remaining simulation time prevents overshooting the endpoint. Exponential local transfers preserve nonnegative inventories, but this does not guarantee temporal accuracy of their coupling. Timestep refinement remains necessary, and strong porosity variation warrants checking the transport estimate against the actual face coefficients.

SLIDE 18
We chose finite volumes because shared face fluxes make local and global fines mass accounting explicit. Mass leaving one cell enters its neighbor, and the outlet flux determines collected fines. Conservative finite differences can be equally appropriate and can yield the same update on a uniform grid. FVM is therefore a transparent implementation choice rather than the only valid method. First-order upwind transport adds numerical diffusion, so grid refinement is still necessary.

SLIDE 19
This is not an artificial numerical separation. The model assumes that hydraulic adjustment is fast enough to neglect a fluid momentum transient. At each time, the current permeability determines the pressure-flow relation. In pressure mode, total resistance gives flow directly. In flow mode, the same resistance gives pressure. PI mode additionally evolves the machine state. Uniform superficial velocity also neglects fluid-volume displacement from porosity changes. This assumption needs assessment if those volume changes become appreciable; exact transient fluid continuity would include storage.

SLIDE 20
The feedback is physical: deposits change resistance, resistance changes flow and pressure gradients, and these alter fines transport and transfer. The equations define this coupling regardless of how we solve them. The code evaluates dependencies in a sequence during each timestep. That numerical ordering introduces discretization error and should be checked by timestep refinement. 

SLIDE 21
We should determine as much as possible independently before fitting the kinetics. Geometry and fluid properties are straightforward inputs. PSD informs fine mass and representative sizes, but the releasable fraction needs separate attention. CT gives porosity and spatial variation, while surface area is sensitive to image resolution and segmentation. Low-flow pressure-flow data constrain permeability. Pore-network calculations could later provide connectivity-sensitive closures. We should not present those future results as available measurements.

SLIDE 22
The experiments should distinguish mechanisms rather than merely provide more fitting points. Time-resolved fines at several flow rates help separate release from capture. Interrupted CT provides spatial evidence that cup mass alone cannot supply. Pressure pulses probe remobilization. We should explicitly compare a simpler one-inventory model with the two-inventory model, and test whether the accessibility factor is needed. These are proposed measurements. Their availability has not been established, and the final evaluation should use withheld cases.

SLIDE 23
These are the new Darcy results with the exponential permeability law and PI controller. A separate Kozeny-Carman run follows in the comparison slides. At 60 seconds the pressure is about 8.99 bar, while flow falls to about 0.045 milliliters per second. The lowest local permeability is about 1.26 percent of its reference value. This shows how the implemented feedback can generate blockage, but the magnitude is strongly closure-dependent and is not an experimentally validated prediction.

SLIDE 24
The fines leaving the puck are divided between the basket and the cup. In the new Darcy example, roughly 0.0614 grams leaves the puck, 0.0215 grams stays at the basket and 0.0399 grams reaches the cup. The split follows directly from the configured constant retention fraction of 35 percent. Cup mass therefore cannot be interpreted as all the fines mobilized inside the puck, or even all the fines leaving it. Some released material remains or is recaptured upstream.

SLIDE 25
These fields show axial deposition and relative permeability in the new Darcy example. They identify an effective region of high resistance within the assumed one-dimensional model. A strong axial weighting in a capture closure could influence this pattern, so we must check it against CT rather than treat it as an independently predicted location. Radial clusters and bypass paths remain unresolved. An especially useful next comparison is between internal redistribution with little outlet mass and sustained transport out of the puck.

SLIDE 26
The blue Darcy-exponential curve overlaps the gray Forchheimer reference. The orange curve changes only the permeability closure to Kozeny-Carman. SLIDE 27
The exponential law produces a minimum relative permeability of about 0.0126. The implemented Kozeny-Carman relation gives about 0.723. The latter therefore predicts much weaker permeability loss for this material-state closure. These are coupled runs: different flow also produces different deposit profiles. The result identifies strong sensitivity to the structural closure, but does not establish which law is physically right. We must compare with measured hydraulic and structural changes rather than tune one closure to match the other.

SLIDE 28
Cumulative cup fines increase from about 0.0399 grams for exponential permeability to about 0.3466 grams for Kozeny-Carman. The basket retention fraction remains 35 percent in both. Higher sustained flow changes release and transport, which changes the collected mass even though the kinetic coefficients are identical. This is a sensitivity result using illustrative inputs. Neither simulated curve is a substitute for experimental calibration and withheld-case validation.

SLIDE 29
The current fifteen unit tests pass. All three comparison runs also pass the mass-conservation and nonnegative-mobile-inventory checks, with relative mass errors below 1.4 times ten to the minus fifteen. The comparison script verifies that only the intended switches differ and that all output times align. These checks support numerical consistency. They do not validate the permeability or fines transfer closures against experiments.

SLIDE 30
Heiko suggests a particularly useful target: conditions where fines move internally but remain trapped, compared with conditions where they leave the puck. Low cup mass alone cannot distinguish no release from release followed by recapture. We need both the inventory history and spatial deposit changes. The closure study is complete. We should next compare operating levels and test the competing closure predictions against measurements. More detailed pore networks or multidimensional models should answer a demonstrated limitation rather than be assumed prerequisites.
